    In real use, photos come only from cameras on city vehicles, and the map is available only to the city and its contractors. Only snow, wet snow and ice in the middle of the sidewalk go on the map.

    How it works

    1. A camera on a bus or sidewalk tractor takes 1–3 frames per second with GPS and time.
    2. A small neural network (YOLO11n) labels the sidewalk: dry, wet, snow, ice or not visible.
    3. The risk score joins the photo with the weather for the next hours: temperature, dew point and precipitation give the chance of ice, wet snow and snow. Black ice is invisible on a photo, so the forecast raises the risk where a wet or dry-looking surface is about to freeze. Bridges and model confidence count too; under 60% a person checks it.
    4. Crews see only what they must clean: snow, wet snow and ice in the walking line, plus the Top 10 places to go first, and a forecast for tonight so gritting can be ordered in advance.
    5. Cleaning check: when a place is marked “treated” and a camera later still sees snow or ice there, the map flags it. This gives the city objective proof of how well the contractor worked.
    6. Next step: a surface temperature sensor next to the camera, so the system knows the real temperature of the sidewalk, not only of the air, and predicts icing for the next night more precisely.
